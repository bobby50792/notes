# 3  Generating Functions



## 3.1 Generating Functions of Sequences



### Ordinary Generating Functions

The ordinary generating function (OGF) of a sequence of real numbers $(a_k)_{k \ge 0}$ is the power series
$$
\begin{align*}
A(x) := \sum_{k \ge 0} a_k x^k.
\end{align*}
$$


###  Tile Covering

Consider a $2 \times n$ rectangle. We want to tile it using $1 \times 2$ dominoes, placed either horizontally or vertically, without overlap and without leaving any square uncovered. Let $a_n$ denote the number of such tilings. The first two values are:

$$
\begin{align*}
a_0 = 1, \quad a_1 = 1.
\end{align*}
$$

What is the value of $a_n$ for general $n$?


$$
\begin{align*}
A(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x - x^2}.
\end{align*}
$$


Then we can decompose $A(x)$ into partial fractions:

$$
\begin{align*}
A(x) = \frac{\alpha}{1 - \varphi x} + \frac{\beta}{1 - \psi x}
\end{align*}
$$

where $(\alpha, \beta)$ is the solution to $\alpha(1 - \psi x) + \beta(1 - \varphi x) = 1$:

$$
\begin{align*}
\alpha = \frac{\sqrt{5} + 1}{2\sqrt{5}}, \quad \beta = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2\sqrt{5}}.
\end{align*}
$$

Expanding each term as a geometric series gives

$$
\begin{align*}
A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \alpha \varphi^n x^n + \sum_{n \ge 0} \beta \psi^n x^n = \sum_{n \ge 0} (\alpha \varphi^n + \beta \psi^n) x^n. 
\end{align*}
$$

Therefore, we obtain that the number of domino tilings of a $2 \times n$ rectangle is

$$
\begin{align*}
a_n = \alpha \varphi^n + \beta \psi^n = \frac{\sqrt{5} + 1}{2\sqrt{5}} \left( \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \right)^n + \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2\sqrt{5}} \left( \frac{1 - \sqrt{5}}{2} \right)^n.
\end{align*}
$$



### Parenthesizing Expressions

Let $a_n$ be the number of ways to fully parenthesize the following expression:

$$
\begin{align*}
x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n.
\end{align*}
$$

Here, "fully parenthesized" means that every addition operation is enclosed in parentheses. Equivalently, $a_n$ is the number of ways to evaluate the expression using only binary additions.


$$
\begin{align*}
a_1 = 1, \quad a_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} a_k a_{n-k} \quad \text{for all } n \ge 2.
\end{align*}
$$


This can be further simplified as the following quadratic equation:

$$
\begin{align*}
A(x)^2 - A(x) + x = 0. 
\end{align*}
$$

Solving this quadratic equation gives

$$
\begin{align*}
A(x) = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4x}}{2}. 
\end{align*}
$$

There are two possible solutions, but since $A(0) = \sum_{n \ge 1} a_n 0^n = 0$, we must choose the minus sign:

$$
\begin{align*}
A(x) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 4x}}{2}.
\end{align*}
$$


### Catalan Numbers

we get

$$
\begin{align*}
a_n = -\frac{1}{2}(-4)^n \cdot \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{2^{2n-1}} \cdot \frac{(2n-2)!}{(n-1)!n!} = \frac{(2n-2)!}{(n-1)!n!}.
\end{align*}
$$


In combinatorics, Catalan numbers, named after the French mathematician Eugène Catalan, are a sequence of natural numbers that appear in many counting problems. These numbers are defined as:

$$
\begin{align*}
C_n = \frac{1}{n + 1} \binom{2n}{n} = \frac{(2n)!}{n!(n + 1)!} 
\end{align*}
$$



###  Summing Variables with Reordering

We ask: Given $n$ variables $x_1, \dots, x_n$, how many essentially different ways are there to compute their sum by repeatedly adding two terms at a time, where the order of operands in each addition is ignored?



Therefore, the correct formula is

$$
\begin{align*}
a_n = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \binom{n}{k} a_k a_{n-k}.
\end{align*}
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
A(x)^2 - 2A(x) + 2x = 0.
\end{align*}
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
{A}(x) = 1 - \sqrt{1 - 2x}.
\end{align*}
$$

Therefore, the number of ways to compute the sum of $n$ variables is

$$
\begin{align*}
a_n = (2n - 3)!!.
\end{align*}
$$


### Exponential Generating Functions

The exponential generating function (EGF) of a sequence of real numbers $(a_k)_{k \ge 0}$ is the power series

$$
\begin{align*}
\widehat{A}(x) := \sum_{k \ge 0} a_k \frac{x^k}{k!}.
\end{align*}
$$


**Theorem 3.1.** Differentiating this equation term by term gives
$$
\begin{align*}
\hat{S}'(x) &= \sum_{n \ge 0} s_{n+1} \frac{x^n}{n!} \\
&= \sum_{n \ge 0} s_n \frac{x^n}{n!} + \sum_{n \ge 0} a_{n+1} \frac{x^n}{n!} \\
&= \hat{S}(x) + \hat{A}'(x).
\end{align*}
$$

So $\hat{S}$ satisfies the linear differential equation
$$
\begin{align*}
\hat{S}'(x) - \hat{S}(x) &= \hat{A}'(x), & \hat{S}(0) &= s_0 = a_0. 
\end{align*}
$$

Multiplying by $e^{-x}$ on both sides gives
$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{dx}} (\hat{S}(x)e^{-x}) = \hat{A}'(x)e^{-x}. 
\end{align*}
$$

Integrating both sides from $0$ to $x$, we obtain
$$
\begin{align*}
\hat{S}(x)e^{-x} = a_0 + \int_0^x \hat{A}'(t)e^{-t} \text{dt},
\end{align*}
$$

where we used the initial condition $\hat{S}(0) = a_0$. Therefore, the EGF of the prefix sums is
$$
\begin{align*}
\hat{S}(x) = a_0 e^x + \int_0^x \hat{A}'(t)e^{x-t} \text{dt}. 
\end{align*}
$$

Equivalently, by integrating by parts, we can also write this as
$$
\begin{align*}
\hat{S}(x) = \hat{A}(x) + \int_0^x \hat{A}(t)e^{x-t} \text{dt}.
\end{align*}
$$


**Definition.** The Laplace transform of a function $f$ is defined by
$$
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}[f](s) := \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st} \text{dt}.
\end{align*}
$$

If the OGF and EGF of a sequence $(a_n)_{n \ge 0}$ are given by
$$
\begin{align*}
A(z) := \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n z^n \quad \text{and} \quad \hat{A}(t) := \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n \frac{t^n}{n!}, 
\end{align*}
$$

then
$$
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}[\hat{A}](s) &= \int_0^\infty \left( \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n \frac{t^n}{n!} \right) e^{-st} \text{dt} \\
&= \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{a_n}{n!} \int_0^\infty t^n e^{-st} \text{dt}. 
\end{align*}
$$
With the change of variables $u = st$, we can rewrite the integral in terms of the Gamma function $\Gamma(z) := \int_0^\infty u^{z-1}e^{-u} \text{ du}$:
$$
\begin{align*}
\int_0^\infty t^n e^{-st} \text{ dt} = \frac{1}{s^{n+1}} \int_0^\infty u^n e^{-u} \text{ du} = \frac{1}{s^{n+1}} \Gamma(n+1) = \frac{n!}{s^{n+1}}, 
\end{align*}
$$

where the last step uses the fact that $\Gamma(n+1) = n!$ for all integers $n \ge 0$.

Plugging this back into the Laplace transform, we obtain
$$
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}[\hat{A}](s) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{a_n}{n!} \cdot \frac{n!}{s^{n+1}} = \frac{1}{s} A \left( \frac{1}{s} \right). 
\end{align*}
$$

Setting $s = \frac{1}{z}$ gives
$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{z} \mathcal{L}[\hat{A}] \left( \frac{1}{z} \right) = A(z).
\end{align*}
$$


### Sum of Powers

$$
\begin{align*}
F_n(x) := \sum_{k \ge 0} S_k(n) \frac{x^k}{k!}.
\end{align*}
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
F_n(x) &= \sum_{k \ge 0} \left( \sum_{j=1}^n j^k \right) \frac{x^k}{k!} 
= \sum_{k \ge 0} \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{(jx)^k}{k!} 
= \sum_{j=1}^n \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{(jx)^k}{k!} 
= \sum_{j=1}^n e^{jx}.
\end{align*}
$$

Of course, we can rewrite $F_n(x)$ as
$$
\begin{align*}
F_n(x) = \frac{e^{nx} - 1}{x} \cdot \frac{xe^x}{e^x - 1}.
\end{align*}
$$

Since $\frac{xe^x}{e^x - 1} = \frac{x}{1 - e^{-x}}$, we can further rewrite $F_n(x)$ as
$$
\begin{align*}
F_n(x) = \frac{e^{nx} - 1}{x} \cdot \frac{x}{1 - e^{-x}}.
\end{align*}
$$

The coefficients of the first term $\frac{e^{nx} - 1}{x}$ are easy to extract:
$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{e^{nx} - 1}{x} = \frac{1}{x} \sum_{k \ge 0} (n^k - \mathbb{1}_{[k=0]}) \frac{x^k}{k!} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{n^{k+1}}{k+1} \frac{x^k}{k!}.
\end{align*}
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
\hat{B}(x) = \frac{x}{e^x - 1}.
\end{align*}
$$

It is easy to check that
$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{x}{1 - e^{-x}} = \frac{-x}{e^{-x} - 1} = \hat{B}(-x).
\end{align*}
$$


$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{x}{e^x - 1} = \sum_{m \ge 0} B_m \frac{x^m}{m!}.
\end{align*}
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
F_n(x) &= \frac{e^{nx} - 1}{x} \cdot \hat{B}(-x) 
= \left( \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{n^k}{k+1} \frac{x^k}{k!} \right) \left( \sum_{m \ge 0} (-1)^m B_m \frac{x^m}{m!} \right).
\end{align*}
$$



**Theorem 3.2 (Faulhaber's formula)**. For all $k \ge 0$ and $n \ge 1$,
$$
\begin{align*}
S_k(n) = \frac{1}{k+1} \sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^j \binom{k+1}{j} B_j n^{k+1-j}.
\end{align*}
$$


## 3.2 Complex Analysis

### Complex Numbers 

A complex number is a number of the form
$$
\begin{align*}
z = x + iy, \quad x, y \in \mathbb{R},
\end{align*}
$$

where $i$ is the imaginary unit. The number $x$ is called the real part of $z$, and $y$ is called the imaginary part. We write
$$
\begin{align*}
\Re(z) = x, \quad \Im(z) = y. 
\end{align*}
$$

The set of all complex numbers is denoted by $\mathbb{C}$.



**Definition.** If $z = x + iy$, then its conjugate is
$$
\begin{align*}
\bar{z} = x - iy,
\end{align*}
$$
and its absolute value or modulus is
$$
\begin{align*}
|z| = (z\bar{z})^{1/2} = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}. 
\end{align*}
$$


**Definition (Complex functions).** Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ be a set of complex numbers. A complex function on $S$ is a function
$$
\begin{align*}
f : S \to \mathbb{C}.
\end{align*}
$$


### Power Series

A power series is an infinite sum of the form
$$
\begin{align*}
\sum_{n \ge 0} a_n z^n, 
\end{align*}
$$
where $a_n \in \mathbb{C}$ is the coefficient of the $n$-th term $z^n$. More generally, a power series with respect to a center $z_0 \in \mathbb{C}$ is an infinite sum of the form
$$
\begin{align*}
\sum_{n \ge 0} a_n(z - z_0)^n. 
\end{align*}
$$


More specifically, $\sum_{n \ge 0} a_n z^n$ means the limit of the partial sums $\sum_{n=0}^N a_n z^n$ as $N \to \infty$, provided that this limit exists in $\mathbb{C}$. That is,
$$
\begin{align*}
\lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=0}^N a_n z^n, 
\end{align*}
$$



A stronger condition is that the series converges absolutely, which means that the series of absolute values converges to finite value:
$$
\begin{align*}
\lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n \ge 0} |a_n z^n| < \infty.
\end{align*}
$$


**Definition (Limits of complex sequences). **We say that a sequence $(z_{n})_{n \ge 0}$ of complex numbers converges to $A \in \mathbb{C}$ if for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that
$$
\begin{align*}
|z_n - A| < \epsilon \quad \text{for all } n \ge N. 
\end{align*}
$$

**Theorem 3.3 (Cauchy–Hadamard theorem)**. Let $\sum_{n \ge 0} a_n z^n$. Define
$$
\begin{align*}
R := \frac{1}{\limsup_{n \to \infty} |a_n|^{1/n}}. 
\end{align*}
$$

Then, for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$,

1. if $|z| < R$, then the series converges absolutely;
2. if $|z| > R$, then the series does not converge.

This $R$ is called the radius of convergence of the power series.



**Theorem 3.4 (Euler's formula).** For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$,
$$
\begin{align*}
e^{ix} = \cos x + i \sin x. 
\end{align*}
$$
Thus The following identity holds: 
$$
\begin{align*} e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0. \end{align*}
$$


Based on Euler’s formula, we can rewrite the polar form of a complex number $z = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)$ into a more compact notation: 
$$
\begin{align*} z = re^{i\theta}. \end{align*}
$$
