# 4 Integrals with Parameters and Improper Integrals



## 4.1 Continuity, Integrability, and Differentiability of Integrals with Respect to Parameters



**Definition.** Let the function $f : [a,b] \times U \to \mathbb{R}$ (where $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$) satisfy the condition that for any $\mathbf{y} \in U$, the function $f(\cdot, \mathbf{y}) : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is Riemann integrable. In this case, we call $\int_{a}^{b} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx$ a **definite integral depending on a parameter $\mathbf{y}$**.

This integral defines a function of $\mathbf{y}$ as follows:
$$
\begin{align*}
F(\mathbf{y}) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx.
\end{align*}
$$


**Theorem 4.1.** Let the function $f : [a,b] \times U \to \mathbb{R}$ (where $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$) satisfy the following conditions:

1. For any $\mathbf{y} \in U$, the Riemann integral
	$$
	\begin{align*}
	F(\mathbf{y}) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx
	\end{align*}
	$$
	exists.

2. $f(x, \mathbf{y})$ is continuous with respect to $\mathbf{y}$ at $\mathbf{y}_0 \in U$, and this continuity is **uniform** with respect to the integration variable $x \in [a, b]$. That is, for any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta(\mathbf{y}_0, \epsilon) > 0$ such that for any $x \in [a, b]$, whenever $\mathbf{y} \in U$ satisfies $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}_0\| < \delta(\mathbf{y}_0, \epsilon)$, we have:
	$$
	\begin{align*}
	|f(x, \mathbf{y}) - f(x, \mathbf{y}_0)| < \epsilon.
	\end{align*}
	$$
	

Then $F$ is continuous at $\mathbf{y}_0$, which implies:
$$
\begin{align*}
\lim_{\mathbf{y} \to \mathbf{y}_0} \int_{a}^{b} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, \mathbf{y}_0) dx = \int_{a}^{b} \lim_{\mathbf{y} \to \mathbf{y}_0} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx.
\end{align*}
$$


**Theorem 4.2.** If $U \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a closed set or an open set, and $f : [a,b] \times U \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, then $F$ is continuous on $U$.



**Theorem 4.3.** Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set. Suppose the function $f : [a,b] \times U \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfies:

1.  For any $\mathbf{y} \in U$, the Riemann integral
    $$
    \begin{align*}
    F(\mathbf{y}) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx
    \end{align*}
    $$
    exists.

2.  The partial derivatives $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_n}$ are bounded on $[a,b] \times U$.

Then $F$ is continuous on $U$.





**Theorem 4.4.** Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set, and let the function $f : [a,b] \times U \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfy the following conditions:

1.  For any $\mathbf{y} \in U$, $f(x, \mathbf{y})$ is Riemann integrable with respect to $x$ on the interval $[a, b]$.

2.  For any $x \in [a, b]$, $f(x, \mathbf{y})$ is differentiable with respect to $\mathbf{y} \in U$.

3.  For each $k = 1, 2, \dots, n$, the partial derivative $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_k}(x, \mathbf{y})$ is Riemann integrable with respect to $x$, and is continuous with respect to $\mathbf{y}$. Furthermore, this continuity is **uniform** with respect to the integration variable $x \in [a, b]$. That is, for any $\mathbf{y}_0 \in U$ and any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta(\mathbf{y}_0, \epsilon) > 0$ such that for any $x \in [a, b]$, whenever $\mathbf{y} \in U$ satisfies $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}_0\| < \delta(\mathbf{y}_0, \epsilon)$, we have:
    $$
    \begin{align*}
    \left| \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_k}(x, \mathbf{y}) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_k}(x, \mathbf{y}_0) \right| < \epsilon.
    \end{align*}
    $$

Then $F(\mathbf{y}) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx$ is of class $C^1$ on $U$, and:
$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_k}(\mathbf{y}) = \frac{\partial}{\partial y_k} \int_{a}^{b} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx = \int_{a}^{b} \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_k}(x, \mathbf{y}) dx.
\end{align*}
$$


**Corollary.** If in the aforementioned theorem, all partial derivatives of $f$ with respect to $\mathbf{y}$ up to order $k$ are continuous with respect to $(x, \mathbf{y})$, then $F$ is of class $C^k$ on $U$. Furthermore, all partial derivative operations of $F$ up to order $k$ can be interchanged with the integral operator:
$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial^k F}{\partial y_{i_1} \partial y_{i_2} \dots \partial y_{i_k}} (\mathbf{y}) = \int_{a}^{b} \frac{\partial^k f}{\partial y_{i_1} \partial y_{i_2} \dots \partial y_{i_k}} (x, \mathbf{y}) dx.
\end{align*}
$$


**Theorem 4.5 (Interchanging the Order of Iterated Integration).** Let the function $f : [a,b] \times [c, d] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous. Then:
$$
\begin{align*}
\int_{a}^{b} \left( \int_{c}^{d} f(x, y) dy \right) dx = \int_{c}^{d} \left( \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) dx \right) dy.
\end{align*}
$$



### Calculus of Variations

The above problem can be summarized as:
$$
\begin{align*}
\min \int_{a}^{b} L(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) dt, \quad \mathbf{x}(a) = A, \mathbf{x}(b) = B.
\end{align*}
$$

$L(t, \mathbf{x}, \dot{\mathbf{x}})$: **Lagrangian function**



Embed the curve $\mathbf{x}(t)$ to be solved into a family of curves $X(t, s)$ (where for each fixed $s$, $X(t, s)$ is a curve) such that:
$$
\begin{align*}
X(t, 0) = \mathbf{x}(t), \quad \forall t,
\end{align*}
$$
and for all $s$, we have $X(a, s) = A$ and $X(b, s) = B$. For example: $X(t, s) = \mathbf{x}(t) + s\mathbf{y}(t)$, where $\mathbf{y}(a) = \mathbf{y}(b) = 0$.

Consider:
$$
\begin{align*}
F(s) = \int_{a}^{b} L\left(t, X(t, s), \frac{\partial X}{\partial t}(t, s)\right) dt.
\end{align*}
$$
Then $F(0) = \int_{a}^{b} L(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) dt$ is the minimum value.



Thus $F'(0) = 0$. Namely,
$$
\begin{align*}
\int_{a}^{b} \left[ L_{\mathbf{x}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) X_s(t, 0) + L_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) X_{s,t}(t, 0) \right] dt = 0
\end{align*}
$$

By integration by parts, the second term becomes:
$$
\begin{align*}
\int_{a}^{b} L_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) d_t X_s(t, 0) &= \left. L_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) X_s(t, 0) \right|_{a}^{b} - \int_{a}^{b} \frac{d}{dt} L_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) X_s(t, 0) dt \\
&= - \int_{a}^{b} \frac{d}{dt} L_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) X_s(t, 0) dt
\end{align*}
$$




Therefore,
$$
\begin{align*}
\int_{a}^{b} \left[ L_{\mathbf{x}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) - \frac{d}{dt} L_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) \right] X_s(t, 0) dt = 0
\end{align*}
$$

Since $X_s(t, 0)$ is arbitrary, we obtain the **Euler-Lagrange equation**:
$$
\begin{align*}
L_{\mathbf{x}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) - \frac{d}{dt} L_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}(t, \mathbf{x}(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)) = 0.
\end{align*}
$$
This is a second-order ordinary differential equation (ODE) with respect to $\mathbf{x}(t)$.



#### Reducible Case 1: $L = L(\mathbf{x}, \dot{\mathbf{x}})$

The Euler-Lagrange equation is:
$$
\begin{align*}
L_x(x, \dot{x}) - \frac{d}{dt} L_{\dot{x}}(x, \dot{x}) = 0.
\end{align*}
$$

Multiplying by $\dot{x}$ and using the chain rule:
$$
\begin{align*}
L_x(x, \dot{x})\dot{x} + L_{\dot{x}}(x, \dot{x})\ddot{x} - \frac{d}{dt} \left( L_{\dot{x}}(x, \dot{x})\dot{x} \right) = 0.
\end{align*}
$$

Since $\frac{d}{dt} L(x, \dot{x}) = L_x\dot{x} + L_{\dot{x}}\ddot{x}$ when $L$ does not explicitly depend on $t$, we have:
$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{d}{dt} \left( L(x, \dot{x}) - L_{\dot{x}}(x, \dot{x})\dot{x} \right) = 0,
\end{align*}
$$

Which leads to the **Beltrami Identity**:
$$
\begin{align*}
L(x, \dot{x}) - L_{\dot{x}}(x, \dot{x})\dot{x} = C.
\end{align*}
$$


## 4.2 Improper Integrals with Parameters

For a function $f : [a, +\infty) \times U \to \mathbb{R}$ (where $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$), the integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx$ is called an **improper integral depending on a parameter $\mathbf{y}$**.



A convergent improper integral with a parameter defines a function with $\mathbf{y}$ as the independent variable:
$$
\begin{align*}
F(\mathbf{y}) = \int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx.
\end{align*}
$$



**Definition.** The improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx$ is said to **converge uniformly** to $F(\mathbf{y})$ on $U$ if for every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $A(\epsilon) > a$ such that for all $A > A(\epsilon)$ and for every $\mathbf{y} \in U$, we have:
$$
\begin{align*}
\left| \int_{a}^{A} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx - F(\mathbf{y}) \right| < \epsilon.
\end{align*}
$$


**Theorem 4.5.** Let the function $f(x, \mathbf{y})$ be Riemann integrable with respect to $x$ on any bounded interval $I \subset [a, +\infty)$. The improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx$ converges uniformly with respect to $\mathbf{y} \in U$ if and only if it satisfies the **uniform Cauchy condition**: for any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $A(\epsilon) > a$ such that
$$
\begin{align*}
\left| \int_{A_1}^{A_2} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx \right| < \epsilon, \quad \forall A_2 > A_1 \ge A(\epsilon), \quad \forall \mathbf{y} \in U.
\end{align*}
$$


**Theorem 4.6.** Let the functions $f(x, \mathbf{y})$ and $g(x, \mathbf{y})$ satisfy:
$$
\begin{align*}
|f(x, \mathbf{y})| \le g(x, \mathbf{y}), \quad \forall x \ge a, \forall \mathbf{y} \in U
\end{align*}
$$
If the integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} g(x, \mathbf{y}) dx$ converges uniformly with respect to $\mathbf{y} \in U$, then the integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x, \mathbf{y}) dx$ converges uniformly (and absolutely) with respect to $\mathbf{y} \in U$.
